---
title: Shine a bright light on the here and now
date: 2026-11-10
tags: reading
---

What we choose to focus on, and what that does to our lives, is a question many philosophers and writers have asked before me.

Here I gather their answers and describe where I see this wisdom at work.

I first stumbled upon the poetry of Lao Tzu – the Tao Te King (or Dao De Jing) – through the book Trust Chaos by Dr. Christian Zippel.

> Those who try to outshine others, <br />
> Dim their own light
>
> Tao Te King - Verse 24

This part speaks to me in particular. I'm often tired of watching friends and acquaintances try to feed their egos by talking about the great things they've bought or own. They often do it believing it makes them a better, more capable person. But the joy is short-lived, because a barrel without a bottom can never be filled. A downward spiral of consumption, compensation and emptiness. Often this behaviour is just a surrogate for what they truly long for. When I bring it up with these people, unexpected depths often open up. They place themselves above others, and those others can feel it. In doing so they deny themselves exactly what they are looking for: connection, belonging and closeness.

In her magnum opus "Finde deinen Fixstern", Vera F. Birkenbihl aptly calls this phenomenon the crowing rooster on the dung heap. A game people play, investing much of their energy into maintaining their self-image and their status.

It reminds us not to focus only on how we want to be seen from the outside. Far more important is being at peace with yourself.
Knowing who you want to be, and who you don't. That takes differentiation and integration: setting yourself apart through your abilities and quirks, and owning them. That also helps you integrate into society as a valuable person.

Just as each part of our body has its own function that complements the others, people have their own function too.
What kind of creature would it be if every organ strived to be like the heart, which so often gets the most credit for keeping us alive?

So it's worth becoming yourself, and not someone else.
Friedrich Nietzsche says as much in Ecce Homo: become who you are.

In the end, it's not just about finding out what you want to be free from, but also what you want to be free for. Ask yourself: do I really want to spend my very limited time on this wonderful planet playing status games and compensating for a poor ego by showing others how great I am? Sadly, that decision is rarely made consciously.

But I think we can cultivate our ego once we see through what it wants to drive us towards. After all, it's human nature to want to be valuable and to be needed. You can let that desire rule you and lead you astray. Or you can put your skills to work doing good. Actions speak louder than words, after all. Let your wisdom express itself in what you do.
If I stop spending my life wanting to be someone else – what do I actually do with this life?

If I free myself from competitive thinking, what do I want to be free for? Your own true self! I'd argue that if you can be yourself, stress-free and authentically, without having to hide from others, you are living a free and self-determined life.

> The greatest life-lie of all is to not live here and now. It is to look at the past and the future, <br />
> cast a dim light on one’s entire life, and believe that one has been able to see something. <br />
> Until now, you have turned away from the here and now and shone a light only on invented pasts and futures. <br />
> You have told a great lie to your life, to these irreplaceable moments.
>
> The Courage To Be Disliked - Fumitake Koga and Ichiro Kishimi

In this book, Alfred Adler's individual psychology points out that you can find your place in the world without having to place yourself above others. In this light, striving for superiority is often just a compensating response to one's own felt inferiority.

There's a beautiful, fulfilling wisdom to take from these books.

Instead of fearing how others see you: meet them. <br />
Instead of wondering whether you are interesting: be interested. <br />
Instead of worrying whether your life is meaningful: do something that gives you meaning. <br />
Instead of comparing yourself to your idealised future self: live with the person you are today.

All of this comes down to one thing. Think less, do more.

Especially me – someone who loves to intellectualise life and every situation to find the optimal wisdom for it – would do well to live more in the here and now.
Overthinking everything doesn't protect us from the uncertainty of what happens when we commit to life. Some things can't be penetrated by thought alone; you have to try them and live them.
